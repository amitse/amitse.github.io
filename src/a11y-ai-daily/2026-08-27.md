## 🚀 Releases & Standards

In [“The 2.1.1 ‘specific timings’ Clause”](https://adrianroselli.com/2026/08/the-2-1-1-specific-timings-clause.html), published 26 August, Adrian Roselli focuses on a qualification that simplified accounts of WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard often leave behind. The criterion says functionality must be operable through a keyboard interface **“without requiring specific timings for individual keystrokes,”** except where the underlying function depends on movement path rather than endpoints.

That language is practical rather than academic. Roselli quotes W3C’s explanation: prohibited timing requirements include having to repeat or execute multiple keystrokes in a short period, or having to hold a key down for an extended period before it registers. He counted only one of ten simplified or plain-English WCAG reinterpretations that attended to the timing language. The conclusion is not that plain-language guidance is a mistake; it is that translation becomes dangerous when it drops the operative condition.

Neither result establishes compliance with the complete keyboard criterion. The missing detail can be the detail that excludes someone.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

Roselli describes opening a Wayfair link in a new tab and encountering an interaction that required holding a key; the desk eventually loaded, but he closed the tab rather than buy it. He identifies this as a WCAG 2.1.1 failure and reports that others discussing the incident initially named other criteria but not 2.1.1. An inaccessible interaction is not merely a failed row in a compliance dashboard. It can be an abandoned purchase or an unfinished task.

The regression-test implication is concrete. Do not stop at “can focus reach the control?” Exercise a normal press-and-release interaction. Check whether it succeeds without a timed hold, burst of repeated keystrokes, or precision sequence. Then test the completed flow at a deliberately slower pace and with the input methods the product expects to support. Automated checks can catch valuable mechanical failures, but they cannot establish every behavioral condition of a criterion like this one.

For AI-generated code, require the system to cite the relevant normative clause in its proposed test plan, then have a person run the test in the product. That workflow turns the model into a drafting aid rather than a compliance authority.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

The argument is not whether WCAG should be comprehensible. It should. The argument is whether an accessibility program can treat a simplified checklist as a replacement for the standard it claims to implement. AI makes that temptation sharper because it can create thousands of tidy recommendations, all confidently labeled with success-criterion numbers.

Roselli’s standard is appropriately unforgiving: **“if you’re going to do the job … you have to do the work.”** The product manager cannot outsource responsibility to a model, the vendor cannot outsource it to a scanner, and the scanner cannot outsource it to a compressed paraphrase. Disabled people encounter the finished interaction, not the reassuring chain of generated artifacts behind it.
