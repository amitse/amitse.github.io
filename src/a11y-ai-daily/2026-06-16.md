## 🚀 Releases & Standards

**EN 17161 and the Limits of What WCAG Can Actually Do**

Deque's Wilco Fiers made the case this week for the European "Design for All" standard EN 17161 as a necessary supplement to WCAG — and the argument is worth sitting with, because it names a structural limitation that automated accessibility tooling won't solve.

The core problem: WCAG's rules must be universally applicable. A test that can fire on every website can't require sign language for educational videos or mandate that a speaker's face remain visible to support lip-reading, because those requirements aren't relevant to most websites. EN 17161's solution is an Integrated Management System (IMS) — a documented framework requiring organizations to identify diverse user needs, involve users directly or indirectly, and measure whether their accessibility efforts are actually effective. Organizations adopting EN 17161 must embed accessibility into the full lifecycle: planning, design, development, procurement, delivery, support, and end-of-life.

This is categorically different from WCAG compliance. WCAG is an output standard: you either meet the criteria or you don't. EN 17161 requires organizations to document the *process* — how they identified needs, how they involved affected users, how they measured outcomes. Fiers connects this to WCAG 3.0's emerging concept of "assertions" — formal claims from an organization that specific disability needs were addressed. He's honest about the tension: *"Asserting you've done something doesn't prove you've actually done it, and there is no way to know if any actions taken were actually effective. There are also liability concerns; organizations may not allow public assertions. And best practices often go out of date quickly."*

The AI connection is direct: automated accessibility engines — axe-core, Playwright a11y, WAVE — can only test what has a deterministic rule. Sign language availability and lip-reading support fall outside that boundary. EN 17161 is an attempt to build a framework for the rest.

**France: Courts Turning the EAA Into Enforcement**

Two actions in the same week out of France give a preview of what European Accessibility Act enforcement looks like when it has teeth. On June 4, the Tribunal judiciaire de Caen ordered Carrefour France to make its online commerce services fully accessible to disabled users, granting the company six months to comply — with daily financial penalties accruing for each day of delay past that deadline. The court was unambiguous about the standard required: "the e-commerce site in question cannot just be somewhat accessible, it must be totally accessible."

On June 2, a coalition of French disability associations — organized through the Fédération des Aveugles et Amblyopes de France — filed a class action lawsuit against the French tax authority, the DGFiP, for the persistent inaccessibility of the impots.gouv.fr portal — the portal French taxpayers need to file returns and manage their accounts.

Deque's Matthew Luken framed this as a structural shift: civil organizations are not waiting for regulators; they are going directly to court to claim their rights. The Carrefour ruling is particularly significant because it applies to a commercial e-commerce service, not a government portal — establishing that private sector digital services carry the same accessibility obligations.

The contrast with the U.S. is now almost cartoonish. The Department of Health and Human Services extended Section 504 web accessibility compliance dates in late May — organizations with 15 or more employees now have until May 2027. While U.S. disability organizations are fighting just to hold a deadline, French courts are setting six-month clocks with daily fines for a supermarket chain. The structural reason: in France, disability federations have civil standing to sue organizations directly, and courts can impose remedies independent of regulatory timelines.

## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

**Roselli Draws the Venn Diagram: Accessibility Has No Overlap with AEO/GEO**

The most important piece of the week is from Adrian Roselli, published June 15, and it is blunt in a way that will save a lot of wasted arguments. As "AI engine optimization" (AEO) and "generative engine optimization" (GEO) emerge as buzzwords — the practice of optimizing websites to appear in and be cited by LLM-generated answers — Roselli published a literal Venn diagram of three circles: Accessibility, SEO, and AEO/GEO. Accessibility and AEO/GEO do not overlap.

His framing is direct: using SEO to justify accessibility was only ever a technique for *"bosses or clients or stakeholders who see accessibility as a cost center."* The AEO/GEO pitch extends this problem. He draws the distinction plainly: while SEO and accessibility share some overlap, and SEO and LLM-optimization share some overlap, accessibility and AEO/GEO are categorically separate — *"accessibility is about people. Twisting a human affordance to feed these corporate efforts to make humans redundant (among all the other harms) is a signal."*

The audiences are categorically different: one is a person who needs your site to work; the other is an extraction system competing with the site owner for traffic. Accessible HTML might help both, but only one of them is the point.

This is the clearest statement yet in specialist accessibility writing that the "AI helps accessibility" narrative needs to be qualified carefully. AI tools can help with specific, bounded tasks — automated rule detection, image description on request, language simplification for cognitive accessibility. They actively produce worse outcomes in others — AI-generated UI components that fail keyboard navigation, auto-captioning without human review, voice interfaces that can't handle augmentative communication devices. Roselli's piece gives practitioners the vocabulary to push back on the conflation.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

**headingoffset Is Not What Developers Think It Is**

Also from Roselli this week: a practical correction aimed at advice spreading in developer venues. The `headingoffset` attribute, a proposed HTML addition, would allow headings to shift their effective level based on their position in the document — a component library author could declare an offset so that a heading inside a card component doesn't collide with the page's main heading structure. What it is *not* is the long-proposed Document Outline Algorithm, which promised that heading levels would automatically resolve themselves from DOM structure and which was never implemented.

Roselli's concern is direct: *"Hi, just me heading off some bad advice I'm starting to see in developer venues."* He's observing that `headingoffset` is being misconstrued as giving developers permission to stop thinking about heading structure. It doesn't. Heading structure is how screen reader users navigate a page — one of the most fundamental accessibility requirements and one of the most commonly broken ones. A new HTML attribute that offers controlled offset within components is a precise surgical tool; it's not a get-out-of-jail card for flat heading hierarchies.

The broader pattern: new HTML features designed to solve accessibility problems in component architecture routinely get misread as simplifications that reduce the developer's obligation to think. They aren't. Roselli's role at this point is essentially a running correction service for that misreading.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

**The AI Errors-Up Problem**

Two things are both true and worth holding together: web accessibility errors are increasing, and AI-generated code is increasing. The 2026 WebAIM Million report (covered in earlier editions) found accessibility errors in the top one million home pages rose approximately 10% between 2025 and 2026. The period of maximum growth in AI-assisted web development runs directly parallel to that increase.

This isn't a coincidence, and it isn't solely AI's fault either. But the defense that "AI tools can help with accessibility" has to contend with this actual outcome. The tools aren't defaulting to accessible output. Copilot-suggested components skip keyboard focus management. Generated forms produce unlabeled fields. AI-written alt text is technically present and semantically meaningless.

Roselli's piece about AEO/GEO and this data point belong in the same frame: the "AI helps accessibility" narrative is winning the marketing argument while losing the technical one. U.S. agencies extend compliance deadlines. Courts in France enforce them. The web gets measurably less accessible while the industry produces more blog posts about AI's potential to fix it.

The honest version of this debate: AI can close specific, bounded gaps in accessibility workflows — automated scanning is better and faster, image description at scale is now feasible, WCAG testing coverage has improved. It is simultaneously true that AI-assisted code generation is introducing new failures at a rate faster than the tooling is catching them. Both things are real. The discourse that treats only one of them as worth discussing is the problem.
