## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

The most substantive piece of accessibility writing to surface this week appeared on Hacker News on June 29. The article is by Sharni Zaugg, published by Infinity Interactive on June 3, and titled ["Read Only. Read Only. Read Only."](https://iinteractive.com/resources/blog/read-only)

It is a detailed first-person account of what happened when Zaugg's consultancy took on a client whose contract specifically required accessibility for blind and deaf employees. The task was a Microsoft Power Automate purchasing approval workflow. The estimate was "a couple hours tops." The reality was far longer.

The client's accessibility specialist — blind herself, and one of the actual users of the workflow — led the review sessions. SharePoint, when sharing a page in read-only mode, appends "(read only)" to every single field. Every one. Zaugg writes: "Imagine watching a film where after every line of dialogue, a voice reads out '(spoken aloud).' You'd lose the plot pretty quickly." For braille reader users it's not just tedious — every redundant character physically crowds out useful content on the device.

Power Automate's native approvals app in Teams wasn't recognized by screen readers at all. JAWS had compatibility issues reading approval emails in Outlook. SharePoint generated multiple H1 headings on a single page. Zaugg explains the significance: "Headings aren't a visual choice for someone who can't see — they're critical to how you move around a page, the same way a sighted person skims bold text to find their place. Two H1s on the same page creates a false landmark. It was disorienting in a way I wouldn't have considered before this."

The workaround, where Microsoft's platform couldn't be fixed: reroute entirely. Instead of asking users to navigate the broken SharePoint tracking page, the system now sends email updates at every stage of the approval. They also wrote honest documentation about what couldn't be changed and how to get through it anyway.

What Zaugg describes isn't a bug report. It's a structural audit of what it means to build on Microsoft's productivity stack when your users are blind. "Most software is built by people who can see and hear, tested by people who can see and hear. That's not an accusation — it's just an honest description of how our industry has operated for a long time." The piece is worth reading in full.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

Manuel Matuzović published ["Your Grid Lanes will likely fail WCAG 2.4.3"](https://matuzo.at/blog/2026/grid-lanes-accessibility) on June 24, the week's most technically precise accessibility analysis.

CSS Grid Lanes — the new layout technique that creates masonry-style columns where the browser decides item placement to balance heights — breaks tab and reading order by default. The browser's placement algorithm optimizes for visual balance, not DOM order. When an item ends up in column 2 visually but appears third in the source, keyboard users and screen reader users encounter a different sequence than sighted users do. That's a WCAG 2.4.3 (Focus Order) failure.

This isn't hypothetical. Matuzović demonstrates that the WebKit team's own Field Guide to Grid Lanes demo would fail WCAG 2.4.3 if it contained focusable elements — and in any real implementation (image galleries with links, card grids with buttons), it will. The `flow-tolerance` property can reduce mismatches in some layouts, but it requires high tolerance values to make a difference, and even then column heights can become imbalanced.

The `reading-flow` CSS property could address many of the issues, allowing authors to declare that focus follows visual order rather than DOM order. But Matuzović notes it "is still theoretical: it's only implemented in Chromium-based browsers and doesn't yet work with Grid Lanes." Safari shipped Grid Lanes without this fix in place. Firefox hasn't published a position.

Adrian Roselli updated his 2015 "Source Order Matters" post on [June 24](https://adrianroselli.com/2015/09/source-order-matters.html) to add a Grid Lanes section — an update that now carries eleven years of source order context directly into the Grid Lanes debate. *(Source: matuzo.at, June 24, 2026; adrianroselli.com, updated June 24, 2026)*

The pattern — a browser ships a compelling layout feature before the accessibility infrastructure is in place — is not new. The difference with Grid Lanes is that its entire value proposition is browser-controlled placement, which makes source order mismatches structurally unavoidable rather than something a developer could simply choose not to do. If you're using Grid Lanes today in production, your keyboard users have a worse experience than you think.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

A small story from the GrapheneOS forum this week, filed June 26, is worth noting precisely because it's not about a controversy.

A blind user posting as "shuteye" thanked the GrapheneOS development team for something most sighted users have never thought about: the ability to set up a new phone independently. Before GrapheneOS added TalkBack to the default install image and engineered a TTS engine with "fastboot" permission — which allows the phone to speak the PIN prompt on reboot — blind users needed sighted help to set up the device. That meant compromising the exact privacy the OS is designed to protect.

"Thank you thank you thank you for finding ways to allow for accessibility in a security-conscious way, so that people with disabilities can also preserve their freedom and data autonomy and are not forced to use the mainstream just because it provides access."

The community response was constructive: forum user "de0u" filed [GitHub issue #1573](https://github.com/GrapheneOS/grapheneos.org/issues/1573) on June 29 to add plain-language onboarding instructions to the web installer, so that future blind users know to hold both volume buttons simultaneously to enable TalkBack during setup. The suggested text: "if you need the contents of your screen spoken, hold down the two volume keys on your device simultaneously until a vibration is felt and the device starts speaking." The naive estimate for the change: two weeks to two months.

The structural tension this illuminates is real: privacy-focused systems have historically treated accessibility and security as competing values. GrapheneOS is demonstrating they don't have to be.
