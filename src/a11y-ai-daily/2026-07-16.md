## 🚀 Releases & Standards

The most significant standards story this week came not from a new spec but from a pairing of two existing ones. On July 9, Wilco Fiers at Deque published a detailed argument for why EN 17161 — a European management standard called "Design for All" — should be treated as the operational complement to WCAG, not a replacement for it. The piece names a real structural failure in how WCAG gets applied.

WCAG's conformance model, Fiers argues, is both its strength and its blind spot. The standard defines conformance at the level of a single web page — you either meet it or you don't. But no real website is ever uniformly conformant. Bugs appear at any level, third-party components introduce failures, and a site that fails three criteria might be excellent or catastrophic depending on which three they are. "The question then becomes, how close to full conformance are you? Unfortunately, WCAG doesn't help us to answer that question," Fiers writes.

His metaphor for the relationship between the two standards is clarifying: "WCAG is the horizon. It is the direction you're traveling in, and even if you can't actually 'reach' the horizon, it's what you're driving toward. EN 17161 is the road you drive on, and it has all the markings, guides, and signs that keep you on track and prevent you from swerving into other traffic or a ditch." EN 17161 introduces the concept of an issue registry — not just a bug list, but a system-wide view of accessibility problems with root cause analysis and accountability structure baked in. For organizations under EAA pressure, the timing is deliberate. [Read on Deque Blog →](https://www.deque.com/blog/why-partnering-en-17161-with-wcag-is-a-major-step-forward-for-digital-accessibility/)

One of three talks was delivered by Cynthia Shelly, who walked through the history and real-world application of accessibility guidelines — including the accessibility story of MSN.com. The post is light on technical detail but signals something important: accessibility is being framed in W3C's current governance conversations alongside human rights and ICT standardization, not just as a technical checklist. ## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

The clearest signal this week came from AccessLumens, a WCAG scanning tool, which published two datasets that amount to pre/post audits of legal deadlines.

The first: a scan of 221 US city government websites serving 50,000+ residents, conducted after the April 24, 2026 ADA Title II web-content compliance deadline. The data shows 42% of scored cities still have at least one critical accessibility barrier after the deadline passed. 45% fail the WCAG success criterion most frequently cited in ADA lawsuits. Notably, 23% of the sites attempted (51 of 221) were protected by bot-walls aggressive enough to block an automated accessibility scan entirely — a detail that should concern anyone trying to measure industry-wide compliance. The average score across the 158 scored cities was 93 out of 100, which sounds reassuring until you read what it means in context: "a city is not optional. If its permit form, tax portal, or council page is unusable with assistive technology, there is no competitor to switch to." [AccessLumens US report →](https://accesslumens.com/research/state-of-us-local-government-accessibility-2026)

The second dataset, published July 13: a scan of 170 major EU e-commerce, banking, travel, and telecom websites across 8 countries, measured one year after EAA enforcement began June 28, 2025. Result: 52.3% still have a critical accessibility barrier. The EAA has been in force for a full year and more than half of the sites it covers still have at least one issue that would block a disabled user entirely. Telecom (66.7%) and banking (55%) fared worst. [AccessLumens EU report →](https://accesslumens.com/research/state-of-eu-ecommerce-accessibility-2026)

These two datasets, read together, tell a story that AI-powered scanning tools are documenting with increasing precision but cannot fix: the compliance frameworks exist, the deadlines passed, and significant failure rates remain. The problem is not measurement anymore. The problem is consequence.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

Adrian Roselli's Link + Popover Navigation post is the most hands-on practitioner content this week. His updated pattern removes the JavaScript dependency from navigation disclosure — a common source of keyboard and screen reader failures — by leaning on the native `popover` attribute. The original 2019 pattern required script to toggle disclosure widget state; the new version does not. Roselli explicitly solicits testing from practitioners to surface gaps before the pattern gets widely copied.

One reader comment in the thread flags a genuine ambiguity: `aria-current` being set twice for different links in the same navigation — a state management edge case that only appears when a user loads a page that sits multiple levels deep in the nav tree. This is exactly the kind of failure that automated scanners miss and that only shows up in real keyboard/screen reader walkthroughs. The thread is worth bookmarking as a working example of public, practitioner-led accessibility review.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

The debate this week is structural, not technical: does legal deadline pressure actually produce accessible technology, or does it produce compliance theater?

The AccessLumens data argues strongly for the latter. Both the US ADA Title II deadline (April 2026) and the EAA enforcement start (June 2025) were major forcing functions — years of advocacy, litigation threats, and public awareness campaigns went into them. The result: 42% critical-barrier rate in US cities, 52% in EU e-commerce. The scanning tools measured everything correctly. The deadlines passed correctly. The enforcement apparatus simply did not materialize at scale.

The counter-argument — that compliance rates would be even worse without the deadlines, and that the baseline data now enables future enforcement — is technically true but emotionally hollow if you were a disabled person who needed a city permit portal to work in May 2026. Legal frameworks operate on timescales that don't match human need.

Deque's Wilco Fiers is making a related but different argument: the tools for measuring compliance (WCAG) were never designed to answer the question organizations are now being legally asked to answer ("are you accessible?"). EN 17161 tries to bridge that gap by building in process accountability — issue registries, root cause analysis, continuous improvement cycles — rather than treating accessibility as a binary state. Whether that framework gets adopted before the next round of legal deadlines is the open question.

The tension here is real: automated scanning tools are producing precise, reproducible failure data, but the institutional response to that data is either slow (government) or cosmetic (legal compliance without genuine usability improvement).
