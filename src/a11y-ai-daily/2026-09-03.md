## 🚀 Releases & Standards

The significant standards-related item in this edition is not a new version of WCAG but a new explanation of how to read the standard responsibly. In [“Evaluating a Success Criterion”](https://adrianroselli.com/2026/09/evaluating-a-success-criterion.html), published 1 September, accessibility specialist Adrian Roselli begins with a problem familiar to anyone reviewing a modern interface against text written before the current pattern existed: intent and meaning can diverge.

His answer is not to improvise a convenient interpretation. Roselli’s sequence starts with the W3C’s normative WCAG text—he notes that WCAG 2.2 is current at the time of writing—then moves to the corresponding Understanding material. From there, he advises readers to examine W3C Techniques with caution, trace GitHub issues and pull requests, look at the small number of WCAG discussions, and weigh commentary from trusted experts that supplies evidence rather than an agenda.

That trail matters because a success criterion is not merely a string that a model can retrieve. It is a requirement with history, explanations, competing readings, and consequences for people using a product. An AI assistant may accelerate the search through that record. It cannot make the record disappear.

## 🤖 AI-Powered A11y Tooling

No new tool announcement is included in this edition. The more useful question is how existing generative tools should be used when a team encounters an ambiguous criterion or a suspected defect.

A model can summarize a success criterion, identify relevant techniques, propose HTML or ARIA, and generate test cases. Those are potentially valuable forms of assistance. But they are not the same thing as an accessibility evaluation. Roselli explicitly warns readers to be cautious with techniques that may be outdated or tied to technologies that have been superseded. That is precisely the kind of context a polished answer can omit while still sounding authoritative.

The practical boundary is simple: treat generated material as a lead, then preserve and inspect the primary evidence. A recommendation should be traceable to the applicable requirement and to the reasoning for applying it to the interface at hand. The faster a tool produces an answer, the more tempting it becomes to skip that second step.

## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

Roselli’s post supplies a better role model for AI-assisted work than the fantasy of automatic conformance. Use automation to reduce the friction of finding documentation and organizing questions; keep interpretation and validation accountable to people who can explain their evidence.

His key instruction is blunt: **“No matter what outcome you find, test everything anyway.”** He gives the reason immediately: releases are constant, regressions are common, support changes, and user expectations shift. A successful answer from an AI assistant cannot establish that a real person can complete a task in the actual product, with the browser, assistive technology, settings, language, and interaction method that matter to them.

This is not an argument for refusing useful tools. It is an argument against laundering uncertainty into confidence. A generated explanation can help a reviewer ask a better question. It should not be mistaken for the answer to whether an experience works.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

For teams building AI-assisted testing or remediation workflows, Roselli’s method suggests a concrete operating standard. Record the criterion under review; link the normative text and supporting material used to interpret it; identify the relevant issues, pull requests, or decisions; document the implementation change; and test the repaired task. That evidence trail distinguishes a reasoned finding from a plausible-looking suggestion.

The need for testing becomes sharper when an apparently official remedy worsens the experience. Roselli writes that a W3C answer to a failure condition can create “a worse experience when remediated.” His point is not that standards may be discarded casually. It is that research makes it possible to justify a departure when one is necessary. A model does not relieve an organization of the responsibility to make, explain, and test that judgment.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

The useful debate is not “AI versus accessibility.” AI can help locate documentation, explain unfamiliar code, and reduce repetitive drafting. The real conflict is speed versus proof.

Speed becomes harmful when it lowers the threshold for declaring an interface accessible. In particular, AI-generated remediation should not be accepted because it resembles a known pattern or cites a criterion. Disabled people experience a workflow, not a diff. A technically tidy repair can still add confusion, friction, or a new barrier.

Roselli’s approach has the stronger claim: take the standard seriously enough to read it in context, and take users seriously enough to test the work. The more fluent our tools become, the more visible that human responsibility should be.

## 📊 TL;DR

Adrian Roselli’s 1 September guide to evaluating WCAG Success Criteria is this edition’s essential reading. It recommends a documented progression from the W3C’s normative text through Understanding material, techniques, issues, pull requests, discussions, and evidence-based expert analysis. Its central instruction—“test everything anyway”—is a direct corrective to AI-assisted accessibility workflows that mistake generated analysis or code for proof. AI can make research and drafting faster; it cannot establish that a real task is usable. The responsible output is an evidence trail and task-level testing, not an unverified declaration of conformance.
