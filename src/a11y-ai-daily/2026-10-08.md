## 💡 How People Are Using Technology for Accessibility

[BIGWORDS.PAGE](https://bigwords.page/) makes a full-screen sign, timer, or message from a link. Its own description says the message is placed after the URL’s `#` fragment; the site says there is no account, no app, and nothing stored on a server. The page demonstrates URL settings for foreground and background colors, font size, slides, timers, QR codes, and animation.

The design is deliberately limited. It is not captioning, sign-language interpretation, or a substitute for an accessible digital channel. A large visual sign cannot meet every person’s communication needs. But the narrow use case is real: a person can put a short message on a phone, tablet, or TV without creating an account or handing the content to a service. The product’s homepage offers examples including arrival signs, countdowns, and Wi-Fi passwords.

The author explained the origin in the project’s [Show HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49994443): they needed to display a message on a remotely managed tablet they could not physically reach, and made the URL carry the message. They also said that URL fragments are not sent to the server, that the project has no backend or storage, and that it is MIT-licensed and self-hostable. Those are author statements, not an independent privacy audit, but they accurately describe the project’s stated model.

A Deaf commenter supplied the essential test that a landing page cannot: a concrete use case. “I’m deaf, so I have to write people all the time with big text,” they wrote, adding that they used a mobile app for this already and saw value in a web-based option. When this edition was checked, the Show HN post had **377 points and 113 comments**. The point is not that a web sign is universally accessible; it is that a specific person identified a specific friction it might reduce.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

The discussion also shows why visual tools need testing beyond a polished demo. One commenter reported a Firefox sizing problem: trailing spaces could make `scrollWidth` exceed `clientWidth` even when the text appeared to fit. The author replied that they had tested only their own browser, then found additional Firefox bugs. That exchange is a useful corrective to the idea that a simple interface is automatically a robust one.

Another commenter raised an epilepsy warning about the site’s “MAGIC” style. The project’s public examples include animation, so motion is not a theoretical edge case. The sources do not establish whether the animation meets any particular accessibility criterion, and this edition makes no such claim. They do establish the practical testing agenda: test real browsers, text fitting, motion, contrast choices, keyboard operation, and whether a hurried person can stop or avoid a distracting display state.

“Large” is not automatically legible, and “private” is not automatically inclusive. A high-contrast default, an obvious pause or reduced-motion path, keyboard-operable editing, and testing with the people expected to use the tool would be more meaningful evidence than a generated assurance that the page looks accessible. AI could help enumerate test states or draft a checklist; it cannot decide whether someone can communicate effectively in the situation that prompted them to open the tool.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

Part of the Hacker News conversation drifted into an argument over whether the phrase “The URL is the app” sounded AI-generated. Several commenters objected to the wording; others pushed back that a stylistic hunch is not evidence of AI use. The dispute is not proof of anything about the project’s authorship, and it should not be treated as such.

It does expose a relevant tension. As generated language becomes common, readers are increasingly wary of smooth claims that leave the actual mechanism vague. BIGWORDS.PAGE is strongest where its boundary is easy to inspect: it renders text and settings encoded in a link. It does not claim to understand a situation, diagnose an accessibility barrier, or replace a person’s judgment.

That is a better model for accessibility automation than “we make your product accessible.” A responsible claim is narrower: a tool can flag a pattern, help draft text, or assist a reviewer in finding a state. The distinction is not semantic fussiness. When an automated system overclaims, disabled people bear the cost of its missed context. When a tool is explicit about its limits, it leaves room for testing, choice, and accountability.
