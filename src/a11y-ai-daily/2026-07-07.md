## 🚀 Releases & Standards

On July 5th, Adrian Roselli published [„Focusgroup Tests"](https://adrianroselli.com/2026/07/focusgroup-tests.html), a set of live accessibility test demos for `focusgroup`, the keyboard navigation attribute that shipped in Chrome 150. `focusgroup` is a proposed mechanism, backed by Open-UI and not yet in WHATWG HTML as anything more than a feature request. Roselli notes dryly that "Open-UI has outsized representation from Google and Microsoft folks, so it's no surprise Chrome would implement it first."

The demos cover the composite ARIA widgets that matter most for keyboard navigation: `listbox`, `menu`, `menubar`, `radiogroup`, `tablist`, and `toolbar`. Each demo contains no scripting, no styles beyond dark/light mode support, and no functionality — by design. The point is to test the bare navigation behavior before a single line of JavaScript muddles the picture.

The reason this matters beyond Chrome 150 trivia is Roselli's explicit framing: *"If you work in digital accessibility, you have an opportunity to find gaps that others might miss (especially in how roles are exposed). Let's get those documented before the spec or browsers ossify code that cannot be readily changed in the future. Let's help ensure developers won't create more inaccessible experiences simply by using this new property."*

That's a specific kind of alarm. Browser vendors implement first, spec catches up later, and once behavior is in the wild and depended on, it's nearly impossible to correct. The history of ARIA is full of examples — role semantics that shipped with quirks and stayed quirky for years. Roselli is inviting accessibility practitioners to do the unglamorous pre-spec testing work that prevents those situations. It requires Chrome 150, which is available today.

The structural tension is worth naming: `focusgroup` is explicitly Google-and-Microsoft-backed technology that will shape how keyboard navigation works across the entire web. The people who understand accessibility best — specialist practitioners, disabled users who depend on these primitives — are not the ones in the Open-UI working groups writing the proposals. They're being asked to test after the fact. That's better than nothing, but it's still a reaction, not a seat at the table.

## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

Sharni Zaugg, a developer at Infinity Interactive, published ["Read Only. Read Only. Read Only."](https://iinteractive.com/resources/blog/read-only) in early June. It surfaced on Hacker News on June 29 and reached 91 points and 81 comments — unusually strong traction for a practical accessibility post. The title comes from a Microsoft SharePoint behavior: when a SharePoint page is shared in read-only mode, it appends "(read only)" to every single field. Every one.

Zaugg had been hired to build a purchasing approval workflow in Microsoft Power Automate. The client had blind and deaf employees who would use it, and had written accessibility requirements into the contract. Zaugg expected a review session or two and a few tweaks. It took 18 hours. Her description of the actual experience of listening to the page through a screen reader is one of the best pieces of accessibility writing published this year:

> "At some point during those calls it hit me that the internet I know — the one I navigate by glancing, skimming, clicking — is almost a completely different place for someone experiencing it through a screen reader. Same web, totally different world."

The list of platform-level failures she encountered is damning. Microsoft's browser apps weren't usable with screen readers; the client had to set up separate desktop app permissions to test at all. Zoom was chosen over Teams and Google Meet because the accessibility specialist "considers Zoom to be the most accessible video conferencing option available." Power Automate's native approvals app in Teams "wasn't recognized by screen readers at all." JAWS, one of the most widely-used screen readers, had compatibility issues reading approval emails in Outlook. SharePoint generated multiple H1 headings on a single page — not a design choice, but a platform default — breaking the heading navigation that screen reader users depend on.

And then the read-only tag problem. On a braille reader, it isn't merely tedious — it physically crowds out useful content. Zaugg looked for a workaround. There wasn't one. "That's just how SharePoint works." She rebuilt the workflow's status delivery entirely, switching to email updates at every approval stage to route around the platform's inaccessibility.

*"I don't say any of this to pile on Microsoft specifically. This is what happens across the software industry when accessibility gets treated as someone else's problem, or a box to check after the fact. It just happens that Microsoft makes the tools a lot of us build on top of, so their gaps become our gaps."*

This connects directly to the EAA enforcement story covered last week. Deque's Matthew Luken documented how monitoring bodies are beginning to trace patterns — automated scanning first, then escalating scrutiny for organizations that fail to respond, up to on-site visits and formal sanctions. But when the non-compliant behavior is in the platform — not in the product built on top of it — enforcement becomes complicated. Who is liable when SharePoint's read-only behavior is the barrier? The organization that deployed SharePoint, or Microsoft? The EAA's second year will likely test that question.

The 81 HN comments are worth skimming. Several practitioners described recognizing the pattern immediately — platform-level failures that absorb hours of developer time and often can't be fixed at all. One commenter noted that the real cost isn't the 18 hours; it's the clients who never had an accessibility specialist on their team, never discovered the problem, and shipped the workflow anyway.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

Roselli's `focusgroup` test suite (published July 5) is the most concrete practitioner contribution of the week. The demos are deliberately minimal — no interactivity, just navigation behavior — which makes them useful for isolating exactly where `focusgroup` does and doesn't behave as expected for specific ARIA roles.

The implicit lesson from the "Read Only" story above applies here too: platform-level testing is different from application-level testing. Automated scanners running against the deployed SharePoint pages wouldn't catch the read-only tag problem — the tags were there, properly labeled, semantically valid HTML. The failure was in the user experience of repetition. JAWS compatibility with Outlook's approval emails wouldn't show up in a WAVE scan. The heading structure issue might surface in an automated check, but the fix wasn't available to the developer.

The gap between what automated testing catches and what actually impairs disabled users is the fundamental challenge of the field. Roselli's explicit call — find the gaps now, before the spec ossifies — is one version of an answer. The other version is what Zaugg did: bring in a blind accessibility specialist as an actual user, record the sessions, and listen to what the screen reader narrates.

W3C's IT Operations team published a note on July 6 about usability improvements to how W3C member organizations manage staff participation in working groups. The post specifically notes the accessibility work done: the filter announces its result count, the summary of pending changes is announced as it updates, and the confirmation dialog follows established dialog behavior. Nothing headline-worthy, but worth noting as an example of documenting accessibility decisions in infrastructure work, not just end-user products.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

The HN thread on "Read Only" (91 points, 81 comments) surfaced a debate that doesn't resolve cleanly: whose job is accessibility when the platform is the problem?

One strand of the argument holds that organizations have a legal obligation to provide accessible experiences regardless of what the underlying platform does. If SharePoint is inaccessible, you shouldn't use SharePoint for accessibility-critical workflows. This is the hard line. It has EAA backing in the European context, where the obligation falls on the deploying organization.

The other strand holds that this expectation is disconnected from how software is actually procured and deployed. Organizations buy platforms — often Microsoft, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Google Workspace — and build on them. They do not typically have the expertise to audit platform-level accessibility, let alone the leverage to demand fixes. The assumption that deployers are responsible for vendor inaccessibility would, in practice, require millions of organizations to either audit Microsoft products themselves or stop building on them.

The real tension is about scale and power. Microsoft's accessibility failures — SharePoint's heading structure, Power Automate's approvals visibility, JAWS compatibility in Outlook — affect every organization that builds on those platforms. A single blog post by a developer at a small consultancy documenting 18 hours of remediation work is doing more to surface those failures than most enterprise accessibility programs. The EAA's enforcement apparatus doesn't yet have a clear answer for platform liability. When a German auditor asks a Dutch retailer why their WCAG 2.1 compliance is weak, the answer "our third-party platform generates multiple H1s" may not be sufficient. But it's also not wrong.

The better argument, for now, is the one Zaugg made: this is what happens when accessibility is treated as someone else's problem. The fix is not to route around Microsoft's failures in perpetuity. The fix is for platform vendors to treat accessibility as a first-class engineering concern, not a box to check in a compliance report. The enforcement pressure from EAA is the mechanism most likely to make that happen — not because it will catch platform vendors directly, but because it will make their downstream customers demand better.
