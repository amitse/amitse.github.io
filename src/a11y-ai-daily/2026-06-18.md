## 🚀 Releases & Standards

**Apple Intelligence Accessibility Features: The Details Matter**

The previous two editions of this newsletter covered Apple's WWDC26 Siri AI announcement in broad terms — the accessibility implications of rebuilding Siri on Gemini, the platform dependency that creates. What hasn't been covered is the substance of what Apple actually announced for disabled users on May 19, and those specifics are worth sitting with.

The headliner is a wheelchair control feature for Apple Vision Pro: using the device's precision eye-tracking system, users with ALS and other conditions that make joystick control impossible can now drive compatible power wheelchairs — specifically Tolt and LUCI alternative drive systems — using only their gaze. Pat Dolan, founder of GeoALS and a member of Team Gleason's patient advisory board, who has lived with ALS for ten years, provided the most direct statement of what this means: *"The option to control my power wheelchair on my own is gold to me."* That's not a marketing quote — that's what assistive technology is for.

Apple also announced on-device generated subtitles for uncaptioned video, rolling out across iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV, and Vision Pro. The key architectural choice is that this runs locally via on-device speech recognition rather than in the cloud — subtitles appear automatically on video that has no captions, including personal clips, family videos, and streamed content. For deaf and hard-of-hearing users, this addresses a persistent gap: professional captioning has expanded dramatically, but the vast majority of personal video is still uncaptioned.

Voice Control receives natural language input, meaning users can describe on-screen elements in their own words rather than memorizing exact labels or numbers. The stated use case — "tap the guide about best restaurants" or "tap the purple folder" rather than "tap element 14" — is real. This also provides a workaround for the scenario where developers fail to properly label interactive elements, since users can describe what they see rather than what the accessibility tree says.

VoiceOver's Image Explorer adds AI-powered detailed descriptions of images systemwide, with the ability to ask follow-up questions in natural language about what's in the camera viewfinder. Accessibility Reader, which reformats content for users with dyslexia or low vision, now handles scientific articles with complex multi-column layouts, adds on-demand AI summaries, and includes translation.

The tension with previous coverage: these features were announced on Apple's platform, controlled by Apple's infrastructure, powered by Apple Intelligence, built on the same APIs that Apple denied to an independent developer named Blake Adams whose WhisperPad dictation app was rejected for "misusing" the accessibility API. Apple's approach to accessibility is described by Tim Cook as "unlike any other." That's accurate. What it is unlike is any model where the platform isn't also the gatekeeper deciding which accessibility use cases are legitimate.

## 🤖 AI-Powered A11y Tooling

**The AI Terminal Problem Nobody Talks About**

In January, a developer writing under the handle xogium published a piece called "The text mode lie: why modern TUIs are a nightmare for accessibility." It arrived on Hacker News in May with 296 points and 137 comments. It has not appeared in this newsletter before. It should have.

The piece documents a specific and compounding problem: modern AI coding tools built on terminal UI frameworks — Gemini-CLI is the primary example, built with Ink (a React-for-terminals framework in Node.js) — are not just inaccessible. They actively harm screen reader users in ways that are difficult to reason about from outside the experience.

The core architectural failure is the distinction between a CLI (a stream model, where output appends linearly and screen readers can parse it chronologically) and a TUI (a grid model, where the terminal window is treated as a 2D canvas of character cells). When a tool like Gemini-CLI "thinks" and updates a status indicator or spinner, it moves the hardware cursor to the timer location, writes the new time, and moves it back. For sighted users, this is invisible. For a screen reader user on Speakup (Linux kernel-level reader), this is what you hear: "Responding... Time elapsed 1s... Responding... Time elapsed 2s... [fragment of chat history]... Responding..." The cursor teleports around the screen. Speakup reads whatever is under the cursor at that exact millisecond.

The piece describes the failure mode directly: *"You end up hearing random bits of conversation mixed with timer updates, making it impossible to focus on what you are actually typing."*

The situation gets worse when pasting text. Because conversation history is part of the application state in these reactive frameworks, pasting a block of text triggers a full re-render. The more messages in the conversation, the more catastrophic the performance degradation. *"Worse, lets pretend that you've somehow managed well with speakup so far, but that you want to do some work with nvda. Maybe paste an error you're getting on windows. So you open your terminal, ssh into your linux box, attach to your screen session and paste your text. The result is an immediate crash of the screen reader (NVDA) or massive system instability."*

This is not a minor friction point. These tools — Gemini-CLI, Claude Code, agentic coding workflows of all kinds — are being positioned as the primary interfaces for a new generation of developer tooling. If they are built on Ink or similar grid-model frameworks, they are categorically inaccessible to developers who use screen readers. The developer who wants to use AI pair programming but relies on NVDA doesn't get a degraded experience. They get a crash.

The fix exists: the stream model (standard stdout, append-only) is accessible by design. The problem is that the frameworks driving terminal UI adoption — Ink, Bubble Tea, tcell — all treat the terminal as a canvas. Every animated spinner, every live-updating token count, every progress indicator shipped in an AI tool is a small accessibility failure that compounds.

The accountability question is pointed: the companies releasing these tools are the same companies announcing AI-powered VoiceOver improvements and wheelchair controls. The right hand is building accessibility infrastructure. The left hand is building developer tools that crash screen readers.

## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

**Darvy: A Blind Founder's Answer to Computer Access**

Majd Darwish, who is blind, shipped Darvy in June — a voice-first AI computer use assistant for Windows, positioned as an alternative to traditional screen reader workflows for getting tasks done on a computer. The pitch is simple: hold the Alt key, describe what you want to do, and the system handles the interaction — reading email, writing to Google Docs, browsing, managing calendar. The AI uses vision capabilities to describe screen state to the user and accepts natural language commands to interact with it.

Darvy ($55/month Personal, $90/month Professional) is early-access software built by one person who has direct stakes in whether it works. That's different from accessibility features shipped as part of a platform strategy.

The conceptual tension with the TUI story above is direct: Darvy is trying to make general-purpose computer use accessible via voice and AI interpretation, while the AI coding tools it might interact with are built on frameworks that actively fail that interaction model.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

**The Platform Problem Isn't Going Away**

Adrian Roselli's piece from June 15 (covered in No. 5) set up the theoretical frame: accessibility is about people, and AI optimization serves different principals. This week's stories give it concrete form.

Apple is the most visible example of the structural contradiction. On one hand: the wheelchair eye-tracking announcement, which is genuinely novel and requires the kind of deep OS integration only a platform owner can provide. Pat Dolan's quote — "gold to me" — is not hyperbole. Vision Pro's eye-tracking precision, calibration-free operation, and range of lighting conditions make it a genuinely competitive assistive device for people with ALS who cannot use joysticks. No third-party developer can build this. Platform ownership is necessary for it to exist.

On the other hand: Apple's App Store rejected a hand-injured developer's dictation app for "misusing" the accessibility API. The same API. The argument Apple uses to justify its accessibility feature investment — tight integration, privacy, consistency — is structurally identical to the argument it uses to prevent others from building on that foundation.

The Carrefour ruling from France (covered in No. 5) points toward the only real check on this dynamic: legal obligation. When courts order Carrefour to achieve 100% RGAA compliance with daily fines, "we already meet 71% of criteria" stops being an acceptable response. The court's framing was exact: "the e-commerce site in question cannot just be somewhat accessible, it must be totally accessible." That standard, applied to platform policies, would look very different from what we have now.
