## 🚀 Releases & Standards

W3C published an update on 12 October saying that [EPUB 3.4 is in the candidate stage](https://www.w3.org/blog/2026/were-on-a-roll-epub-3-4-is-coming/), alongside an update to EPUB Accessibility 1.2. The candidate stage matters: W3C says it is testing the specification, seeking implementer feedback, and ensuring revisions are solid before final publication. It is a procedural detail that rarely attracts launch-day excitement, but it is how accessibility becomes less dependent on which reader, platform, or vendor happens to be in the room.

The headline feature is a new `rendition:layout` value, `roll`, intended for vertically read webtoons. W3C describes these comics as “one long, vertical strip of images,” a form built around phones and previously confined to specialist apps or sites. EPUB 3.4 also refines accessibility metadata, adds an accessibility contact-email field, supports newer image, audio, and video formats, and is supported by EPUBCheck 5.4.0.

There is a warning inside the good news. W3C explicitly says roll layouts inherit “the same accessibility challenges as fixed layout EPUBs.” That caveat should be the headline for anyone imagining that generative AI will flood publishing with automatically produced comics, textbooks, and multimodal documents. A file that validates is an important baseline; it is not proof that a blind reader can understand a sequence of images, navigate a lesson, or receive an adequate textual alternative. AI can make publishing much faster. It can also make inaccessible publishing much cheaper to mass-produce. The standard creates a place to record accessibility information; it does not manufacture the human judgment needed to provide it.

## 🤖 AI-Powered A11y Tooling

On 9 October, [Deque announced that Gartner had named it a Leader in the inaugural 2026 Magic Quadrant for Digital Accessibility](https://www.deque.com/blog/deque-named-a-leader-in-the-2026-gartner-magic-quadrant-for-digital-accessibility/). Read this with the usual care owed to a vendor announcement: this is Deque’s account of its placement, and the Gartner report itself was not independently reviewed for this edition.

The announcement describes Deque’s Axe MCP Server, AI-driven analysis, remediation suggestions, and “agentic and human-in-the-loop” workflows as part of its pitch. Preety Kumar, Deque’s CEO and founder, says accessibility is “an increasingly important engineering discipline as regulation expands and the pace of software development accelerates.” That is the core commercial argument for AI-assisted accessibility: where code is generated, reviewed, and merged faster, accessibility feedback must appear inside those same workflows or it will be perpetually deferred.

But the strongest passage in Deque’s statement is also the least convenient for AI boosters: “Knowing when to use an automated Intelligent Guided Test, a deterministic test, or manual testing is the real differentiator.” Exactly so. The goal cannot be a chatbot that makes a pull request look conscientious. It must be a system that distinguishes mechanical failures from questions about a user’s task, comprehension, control, and safety. A tool can identify a missing accessible name; it cannot, by its scan alone, establish that a newly generated checkout flow is understandable to a screen-reader user trying to buy medicine under time pressure.

The economic tension is straightforward. AI agents make it easier to ship UI at a volume teams may not be staffed to review. Accessibility vendors therefore have a powerful incentive to sell automation that scales at the same rate. The ethical requirement is that they not confuse scale with assurance. Deque’s human-in-the-loop language is the right constraint; customers should treat it as a contractual expectation rather than a marketing flourish.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

Nothing notable in the past seven days beyond the testing implications of EPUB 3.4 and the continuing shift of accessibility checks into development workflows.

For practitioners, EPUB 3.4’s message is practical. Run EPUBCheck 5.4.0, but do not stop there. Test a real book in actual reading systems, including navigation, reading order, image descriptions, media alternatives, zoom or reflow behavior where applicable, and keyboard or assistive-technology use. Fixed layouts and image-heavy vertical comics demand especially skeptical testing because formal conformance can coexist with a reading experience that supplies almost none of the meaning encoded visually.

The parallel for AI-generated application code is direct. Automated checks belong in CI and code review because they catch repeatable faults earlier and cheaper. They should be paired with task-based testing before a release is considered accessible. The test question is not “did the agent fix the ticket?” It is “can the person accomplish the thing the product exists for, using the technology they rely on?”

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

This week’s argument is not really whether AI can help accessibility. It plainly can help teams find recurring defects, explain rules, draft remediation, and surface information where developers work. The argument is who bears the cost when that help is wrong.

If an AI-generated fix is misleading, the developer may lose time. If it creates a confusing experience in a banking, health, education, or government service, disabled people may lose privacy, money, independence, or access to a public right. That asymmetry is why “human in the loop” cannot mean a rushed approval click by someone with no testing time. It has to mean an accountable reviewer with authority to block a release and a process that includes disabled users’ evidence.
