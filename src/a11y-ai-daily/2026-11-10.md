# ♿ Accessibility × AI Daily — 10 November 2026

This week’s useful accessibility-and-AI story is deliberately unglamorous: the paperwork. In [new guidance on choosing a VPAT approach and using AI in an ACR workflow](https://www.deque.com/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-vpat-approach-for-your-organization/), published November 4, Deque makes an important distinction. AI may help make conformance reporting faster, clearer and more consistent; it should not be asked to substitute for testing, remediation, validation or a conformance judgment.

That boundary matters because a VPAT-based Accessibility Conformance Report (ACR) is more than polished procurement prose. It is a statement about what a product does and does not support. A document produced from weak evidence can look authoritative while concealing the uncertainty that a buyer needs to understand. The risk is not that generated language is always wrong; it is that fluent language can make an untested conclusion look settled.

## 🚀 Releases & Standards

Deque’s guidance is unusually direct for a vendor publishing in a market eager to automate compliance work. “**When you’re talking about high-risk areas such as security, privacy, or accessibility, you can’t afford anything less than trustworthy, accurate results—something AI is notoriously poor at delivering,**” the article says.

The article does not argue that AI has no role in a VPAT or ACR. Its position is narrower and more useful: AI should assist people and deterministic tools, not replace them. That is a meaningful standard for conformance reporting. The question is not whether a model can fill a template; it is whether every consequential statement in that template remains connected to evidence about the product.

## 🤖 AI-Powered A11y Tooling

Deque identifies several bounded uses for AI: detecting inconsistencies, catching errors such as text placed in the wrong field, comparing results between versions, finding stale copyright information or misspellings, and rewriting rough prose in clearer language. These are sensible assistant tasks. They make a reviewer more effective without asking a system to invent the underlying assessment.

The source also sets out the technical limit clearly. General AI tools cannot be relied on for comprehensive accessibility testing when they are typically limited to code analysis and do not have real browser rendering, runtime ARIA states or computed contrast. Even automated accessibility tests require human review. And because many WCAG success criteria depend on subjective context, Deque says general AI cannot reliably assess conformance on its own.

The temptation to cross that line is obvious: a model can turn audit notes into a plausible-looking report in seconds. But a plausible report is not an evaluated experience. The useful measure for an AI accessibility product is therefore not whether it produces confident text; it is whether a practitioner can trace that text back to the relevant test result, product version and review decision.

## 💡 How People Are Using AI for Accessibility

The article cites a Deque case study of an agentic remediation workflow integrated with Axe MCP Server. Deque reports that the workflow was applied to 171 WCAG 2.2 AA issues; 80% of applicable issues were remediated and validated end-to-end, with 70% requiring no human edits and 10% requiring light human review. The company also reports remediation was 98% faster overall and estimates more than $25,000 in engineering-cost savings.

Those figures are promising, but their meaning is specific. They describe a reported workflow for a defined set of applicable issues; they do not establish that an entire product conforms. The distinction should be kept intact, especially when automation is doing the work that is easiest to count. The unresolved cases are often where context and expert judgment matter most.

There is a constructive version of AI assistance here: use it to help people inspect evidence, compare changes and express findings clearly. Do not let it become the evidence or the final authority. That principle respects both the potential of automation and the people who have to live with the consequences of an inaccurate accessibility claim.

## 🐛 Testing & Bug-Fixing in Practice

For teams preparing a VPAT, the practical lesson is to keep the reporting stage downstream of the assessment. Accessibility testing must be able to observe the running interface; remediation must be checked; and the resulting conformance statements must be reviewed by someone who can interpret the product and the relevant criteria.

AI can assist at the edges of that process. A comparison tool can flag a changed result. A language tool can help make a technical explanation readable. An automated workflow can accelerate a repeatable remediation task. But a human reviewer needs to retain responsibility for the support status and the explanation that accompanies it. Speed is valuable only if it preserves the chain from claim to evidence.

Deque says that ACR generation in Axe Monitor can produce documentation up to 90% faster than traditional processes. The responsible interpretation is not “90% less accessibility work.” It is an opportunity to spend less time recreating documentation and more time on testing, review and honest reporting.

## 🔥 Discourse & Debate

The argument is often framed as humans versus AI. That is the wrong contest. The real tension is between accountable accessibility evidence and scalable reassurance.

Organizations understandably want rapid answers to procurement questionnaires, and automation can reduce a genuine administrative burden. But speed becomes harmful when it shifts the work from evaluating an interface to generating confidence about it. Accessibility conformance is not an output format. It is a claim about whether people can use a product, and the claim should remain answerable to actual evaluation.

The better position is not to ban AI from accessibility work. It is to refuse its elevation from assistant to authority. Let it find a mismatch, compare versions and improve prose. Do not ask it to turn missing evidence into a conformance conclusion.
