## 🚀 Releases & Changelogs

GLM 5.2 is the model story of the week, and the Semgrep team lit the fuse. Their benchmark post — "GLM 5.2 beats Claude in our benchmarks" — landed on HN with 479 points and 236 comments on June 28. The specifics matter: GLM 5.2 is priced at roughly $1.40 per million input tokens and $4 per million output tokens. Anthropic's Opus 4.x line costs $5/M input and $25/M output. That's more than a 6x gap on output pricing, and Haiku 4.5 — the only Anthropic model even in the same price tier — comes in at $1/M input and $5/M output, still outpriced on outputs. As Amol Kapoor put it in his [Tokenmaxxing piece on 12 Grams of Carbon](https://12gramsofcarbon.com/p/agentics-tech-things-tokenmaxxing) (June 27): *"GLM 5.2 blows Haiku out of the water, and in some cases is even stronger than GPT 5.5 on benchmarks. If I were the big labs, I'd be pretty concerned about this."* Semgrep's cybersecurity benchmark is domain-specific, and nobody should extrapolate too fast — but the direction is clear. Open-weight and Chinese-lab models are no longer running a lap behind. The pricing pressure on Anthropic and OpenAI's mid-tier products is real and structural.

VS Code 1.126 shipped June 24 and it's quietly one of the more consequential feature releases in months. The headliner is multi-agent session management: the Agents window can now hold several chats at once sharing the same working context, with tabs you can rename and switch between. Less flashy but more significant: comments you leave on generated code are now stored on the agent host server, not in your local session — meaning the agent can interact with your review feedback even when the client is disconnected. The session token cost now shows whole-session totals, not just per-turn, which makes burn rates visible in a way that will change how people prompt. The Trust Parent button in the Workspace Trust editor was also removed after it was identified as too easy to accidentally click, trusting more folders than intended. VS Code has stopped pretending agents are a feature and started treating them as the primary mode of operation.

### Claude Code CLI

[Claude Code](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/releases) shipped three releases this week (v2.1.191 through v2.1.195, June 24–26), adding up to a substantial polish pass on the agent infrastructure. v2.1.191, released June 24, delivered the 37% CPU reduction in streaming by coalescing text updates to 100ms — that was covered in the last edition. The auto-submit for languages written without spaces (Japanese, Chinese, Thai) now fires correctly. These are the patches you only write after you've watched enough real workflows break.

## 💡 How People Are Using It

The most-discussed AI dev story on HN this weekend wasn't a tool release. It was a developer named Antoine who used Claude Code with Opus 4.8 to [analyze his own shoulder MRI](https://antoine.fi/mri-analysis-using-claude-code-opus) — 358 points, 463 comments as of June 28. The setup: a 266 MB DICOM export, a fresh Claude Code session with `xhigh` compute budget, and the instruction "right shoulder pain for 2–3 weeks." The human orthopedist diagnosed a Grade III (>50%-width) partial-thickness tear at the apical insertion of the subscapularis tendon and immediately started treatment. GPT-5.5 Pro flagged both the shockwave therapy and the Traumeel injection as potentially inappropriate. Opus 4.8 did something more striking: it reported the tendon as intact.

What makes the story worth reading is how it lands. Antoine isn't triumphant. He writes: *"My hope is that in a couple of model generations, we'll trust AI to review MRIs the way we trust it to proofread our emails."* The resolution — after running a multi-subagent arbitration pass with fresh context — was that the AI evidence favored no tear. He's left in "a state of limbo." The 463-comment thread is mostly thoughtful: the diagnostic conflict is unresolved, no ground truth has been established, and the author is honest about not knowing who to trust. What the piece actually demonstrates is the gap between Claude Code's engineering capabilities (it ran hour-long analyses, used medical imaging libraries, spun up parallel subagents) and the epistemic weight you can place on the results. The tool worked exactly as designed. Whether the output is reliable is a different question entirely.

The tokenmaxxing debate connects here. Amol Kapoor's [June 27 Substack piece](https://12gramsofcarbon.com/p/agentics-tech-things-tokenmaxxing) argues that raw token-stuffing as a strategy is dying — context windows are large enough that it no longer moves the needle to blindly maximize context — but that token-heavy *agentic pipelines* are the new form of the same behavior. The MRI analysis is a perfect illustration: Opus spent an hour running code, processing images, spawning subagents. That's not a prompt, it's a job. The cost at `xhigh` is real. The interesting question Kapoor poses is whether the ROI math eventually closes: *"The natural end state of all of this is the 'software factory' or, even further, the 'dark factory' — a codebase that pumps out code, reviews code, fixes bugs, writes tests, and so on without any human supervision."* StrongDM is already arguing engineers should target $1,000/day in token spend. Kapoor thinks that's hype. The trajectory is real even if the number is absurd.

## 🔥 Discourse & Drama

The big structural story this week is government control over model access, and it's moving fast. Claude Mythos 5 was unblocked by the US government on Friday, June 27. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick sent a letter to Anthropic's chief compute officer Tom Brown stating that "appropriate safeguards are in place to permit certain trusted partners to access the Claude Mythos 5 Model." More than 100 US institutions — major companies and government agencies — now have access. Fable 5, the consumer-facing model, remains blocked with talks ongoing.

The same week, GPT-5.6 (Sol, Terra, Luna) launched in limited preview — but only to a small group of "trusted partners whose participation has been shared with the government." OpenAI's own announcement said they don't believe "this kind of government access process should become the long-term default." The Trump administration came into office promising a laissez-faire approach to AI and is now functionally deciding who gets frontier model access before public release. The structural tension is obvious: government-gated AI access is government-gated competitive advantage. Which companies are in the trusted partner cohort? Unknown. Which industries benefit most? Whoever got the call.

Separately: the long-running [OpenAI Codex issue #2847](https://github.com/openai/codex/issues/2847) — a request for a way to exclude sensitive files — surfaced on HN with 179 points and 120 comments on June 28. The request is straightforward: a `.codexignore` at repo and global level, so agents never read `.env`, `*.pem`, `id_*`, `.aws/**`, `.ssh/**`. The issue was opened in August 2025. It's still open. The security implication isn't theoretical — agentic coding tools with filesystem access and no file exclusion mechanism are sending whatever they can read to the model. The Claude Code team has spent the last two weeks hardening enterprise permission controls; the Codex issue tracker has a 10-month-old open request. That gap is a real product story.

Finally, "We need tech news sources which exclude AI" posted to HN on June 28 with 81 points and 41 comments. The post itself is tired frustration. The top reply is the more interesting artifact: *"I've been coding professionally for over 30 years. LLMs have changed the profession more in the last 12 months than anything else has in all that 30 years."* The ask to exclude AI coverage from tech news is the same impulse as wanting to exclude electricity coverage from industrial news circa 1910. The tools are everywhere. The complaints are, too.
